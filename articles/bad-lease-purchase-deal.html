How to Spot a Bad Lease-Purchase Deal

Every driver has heard the pitch. Own your own truck. Be your own boss. No credit check, no money down, just sign here and start rolling. It sounds like the shortcut to running your own operation. Sometimes it is. A lot of the time it isn't.

We're not here to tell you every lease-purchase program is a scam. Some drivers make them work. But plenty of drivers have signed one, run hard for months, and walked away with nothing but a pile of deductions and no truck. Here's how to tell the difference before you put your name on anything.

Know What You're Actually Signing

A lease-purchase means you're leasing a truck, usually from the carrier or a company tied to the carrier, with the option or the promise to own it at the end. While you're leasing, you're typically an independent contractor. That means you're paying for things a company driver never sees: fuel, maintenance, insurance, tires, and more.

The big problem? In a lot of these deals, the same company controls your truck payment and your freight. If they slow your loads down, you still owe the payment. That's the setup you need to look at hard.

Red Flags That Should Stop You Cold

  • They won't give you the contract to take home. A fair deal can survive you reading it overnight. If they want it signed today, in the office, with a recruiter standing over you, walk.
  • No guaranteed miles or freight in writing. Verbal promises about "plenty of freight" don't pay a truck note. If it isn't in the contract, it doesn't exist.
  • A big balloon payment at the end. Some deals have you paying weekly for the whole term, then owing a large lump sum to actually take the title. Find out exactly what you owe on the last day.
  • You can't run for anyone else. If you're "independent" but can only haul their freight, on their schedule, under their rules, you're carrying owner-operator costs without owner-operator freedom.
  • Termination wipes you out. Read what happens if you quit, get hurt, or get let go. In bad deals, you lose the truck and every dollar you've put into it.
  • Escrow and maintenance accounts with fuzzy rules. If they hold back money from every settlement, you need to know when and how you get it back. Vague answers are a bad sign.
  • Old, high-mileage equipment at a premium price. Ask the year, the miles, and the repair history. A tired truck breaks down, and in a lease-purchase you're usually the one paying for that downtime.

Questions to Ask Before You Sign

Get answers in writing. If a recruiter can't or won't answer, that tells you something too.

  1. What is the total amount I will pay over the full lease, including any final payment?
  2. What gets deducted from my settlement every week, and what's each deduction for?
  3. Who pays for major repairs, and what happens to my payment while the truck is in the shop?
  4. Is freight or a minimum number of miles guaranteed? Where is that in the contract?
  5. Can I haul for other carriers, or am I locked in?
  6. What happens to my equity if I leave early or the lease is terminated?
  7. Can I talk to drivers who have finished this program and own their truck today?

That last one matters. If they can't point you to drivers who actually made it to the title, ask yourself why.

Run the Numbers Yourself

Don't trust a recruiter's sample settlement sheet. Those are usually built on a good week. Take the contract and figure out what a slow week looks like. A week with a breakdown. A week where you take home time. Add up the truck payment, insurance, fuel, maintenance reserve, and every other deduction, then see what's left.

If the deal only works when everything goes right, it's not a good deal. Trucking doesn't go right every week.

Get a Second Set of Eyes

Before you sign, have someone who doesn't work for the carrier read the contract. That could be an attorney, an accountant who knows trucking, or an experienced owner-operator you trust. A little time up front can save you a whole lot of money and grief down the road.

Consider the Company Driver Route First

There's no shame in staying a company driver while you build your experience, your savings, and your credit. You get a steady check, the carrier carries the truck costs, and you learn how the freight moves. When you're ready to own, you'll go in with your eyes open and money in the bank instead of leaning on a deal built to keep you in debt.

A lot of drivers get pushed toward lease-purchase because it's the easiest offer to find, not because it's the best one. The right company driver job can put more in your pocket with a lot less risk.
