CDL-A Pre-Trip Inspection Basics

Before you leave the yard, federal rules already say you have to be satisfied the truck is safe to drive, review the last inspection report when one is required, and not roll a vehicle that is likely to cause an accident or a breakdown. This guide walks through those rules in plain English for property-carrying CDL-A drivers.

It is general guidance, not legal advice and not a substitute for your carrier's inspection procedure or the vehicle inspection you practiced for your CDL skills test. When a step comes from a federal rule, we link the official eCFR source. The current text of these sections includes amendments published February 19, 2026.

What You Have to Do Before You Drive

Under 49 CFR 396.13, before driving a motor vehicle, the driver shall:

  • Be satisfied that the motor vehicle is in safe operating condition.
  • Review the last driver vehicle inspection report if required by 396.11(a)(2)(i).
  • Sign the report to acknowledge that the driver has reviewed it and that there is a certification that the required repairs have been performed. The signature requirement does not apply to listed defects on a towed unit which is no longer part of the vehicle combination.

Those reports may be created and maintained in electronic format under 49 CFR 390.32, which 396.13(d) points to.

Separately, 49 CFR 392.7(a) says no commercial motor vehicle shall be driven unless the driver is satisfied that the listed parts and accessories are in good working order, and the driver has to use them when and as needed.

The Parts and Accessories the Rules Name

Section 392.7(a) and the driver vehicle inspection report rule in 49 CFR 396.11(a)(1) name the same core list:

  • Service brakes, including trailer brake connections
  • Parking (hand) brake
  • Steering mechanism
  • Lighting devices and reflectors
  • Tires
  • Horn
  • Windshield wiper or wipers
  • Rear-vision mirror or mirrors
  • Coupling devices
  • Wheels and rims
  • Emergency equipment

That is the minimum the federal rules name. Your carrier may require a longer checklist, and the method you use to inspect each item should follow the carrier's procedure and the inspection section of your state's CDL handbook. This page is not a walk-through of how to inspect a brake chamber or measure tread depth.

49 CFR 392.8 adds that no commercial motor vehicle shall be driven unless the driver is satisfied that the emergency equipment required by 49 CFR 393.95 is in place and ready for use.

If you hook intermodal equipment, such as a chassis, 392.7(b) has a separate list you have to be satisfied is in good working order before that equipment is operated over the road. It includes visible service-brake components, lighting and conspicuity markings, wheels, rims, lugs and tires, air-line connections, the king pin, rails or support frames, tie-down bolsters, locking devices and sliders. Drivers who accept the equipment and take it over the road are deemed to have confirmed those components were in good working order.

The Driver Vehicle Inspection Report

At the end of the day, 49 CFR 396.11(a) covers the written report often called a DVIR.

Every motor carrier has to require its drivers to report, and every driver has to prepare a written report at the completion of each day's work on each vehicle operated, except for intermodal equipment tendered by an intermodal equipment provider. The report has to cover at least the parts listed above.

The report must identify the vehicle and list any defect or deficiency discovered by or reported to the driver which would affect the safety of operation of the vehicle or result in its mechanical breakdown. If you operate more than one vehicle during the day, a report must be prepared for each vehicle operated. Drivers are not required to prepare a report if no defect or deficiency is discovered by or reported to the driver. That exception is in 396.11(a)(2)(i).

When a report is prepared, the driver must sign it. On two-driver operations, only one driver needs to sign, provided both drivers agree as to the defects or deficiencies identified. The report may be created and maintained electronically under 49 CFR 390.32.

Before a carrier can require or permit a driver to operate the vehicle again, it or its agent has to repair any listed defect or deficiency that would be likely to affect the safety of operation, and certify on the report that the defect has been repaired or that repair is unnecessary. Carriers have to keep the report, the certification of repairs, and the certification of the driver's review for three months.

These DVIR rules do not apply to a private motor carrier of passengers (nonbusiness), a driveaway-towaway operation, or a motor carrier operating only one commercial motor vehicle, per 396.11(a)(5). If you drive for a one-truck carrier, ask how they want defects documented so you are not guessing. Intermodal equipment has its own reporting path in 396.11(b) when the equipment is returned to the provider.

If You Find a Defect

Do not treat a known safety defect as something to “run out.” Under 49 CFR 396.7(a), a motor vehicle shall not be operated in such a condition as to likely cause an accident or a breakdown of the vehicle. Section 396.13 still requires you to be satisfied the vehicle is in safe operating condition before you drive. If a defect would affect safety, it belongs on the report, and the carrier has to address it before the vehicle is operated again.

If an unsafe condition is discovered while the vehicle is already being operated on the highway, 396.7(b) allows the vehicle to be continued in operation only to the nearest place where repairs can safely be effected, and only if that is less hazardous to the public than leaving the vehicle on the highway.

Write down what you found, tell the carrier or the shop the way they require, and do not sign off that a repair was done unless the certification is actually there. The next driver is counting on that report. If you are reviewing a prior report that listed defects, 396.13(c) says you sign to acknowledge you reviewed it and that there is a certification that the required repairs have been performed.

The CDL Skills-Test Inspection Is Not the Same as the Daily Rule

When you tested for your CDL, the skills test included a pre-trip vehicle inspection. Florida's official Commercial Driver License page lists that inspection as one of the three skills tests for a Class A license, along with basic vehicle control and the on-road test. The official Florida CDL Handbook (PDF) is the study source for how that test is administered in Florida.

That test is how the state checks that you know how to inspect a vehicle. The daily federal duty in 392.7, 396.13 and 396.11 is what you have to do on the job, in the form your carrier uses. Use both: the handbook for the method you were trained on, and the federal sections above for the legal duties and the DVIR.
