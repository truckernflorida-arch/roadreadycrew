What to Bring to CDL-A Carrier Orientation

You got the call, and now the carrier wants you at orientation. That's a good sign, but it is also the point where a lot of drivers get surprised — by a document they didn't bring, a test they weren't expecting, or a job that doesn't match what the recruiter described.

This guide covers what CDL-A drivers typically need to bring, the hiring checks federal rules already require, and the questions worth getting in writing before you spend a day (or a bus ticket) on orientation. It is general guidance, not legal advice and not a substitute for the carrier's own instructions. When a step comes from a federal rule, we link the official source.

Documents and Credentials to Bring

The exact list comes from the carrier, so ask for it in writing before you go. These are the items almost every CDL-A orientation will involve:

  • Your valid CDL. It should be issued by the U.S. state where you live. If you have endorsements the job needs — hazmat, tanker, doubles — they should already be on the license.
  • Your current DOT medical examiner's certificate. Carriers cannot put you in a commercial motor vehicle without a current medical card. Bring the card itself, not a photo of an expired one. If yours is close to expiring, get a new exam before you travel. We cover who can do the exam and how long the card lasts in our DOT medical card basics guide.
  • Documents for employment eligibility. Once you are hired, the carrier has to complete Form I-9, the federal employment-eligibility form. They will tell you which documents they accept. Do not send your Social Security number, date of birth or license number to Road Ready Crew; those go only on the carrier's own official application. The official list of acceptable I-9 documents is on USCIS's Form I-9 page.

If the carrier asked for anything else besides those items, bring what they named. If they did not send a list, ask for one before you leave home.

Checks the Carrier Will Typically Run

Background, drug testing and a road test are on the carrier, not on us. Federal rules already require several of these steps before you can drive for them:

  • A motor vehicle record (MVR) inquiry. Under 49 CFR 391.23, a carrier has to request your driving record as part of the pre-employment investigation. The details are in our MVR requirements for CDL drivers guide.
  • A Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse full query. Before you perform safety-sensitive functions, the carrier has to run a full query and you have to give electronic consent, per 49 CFR 382.701. That means you should already be registered in the Clearinghouse so you can consent. See our Clearinghouse guide and the official FMCSA Clearinghouse site.
  • A pre-employment drug test. Part 382 requires a negative pre-employment test before you drive. How and where you test is set by the carrier. Ask when and where it happens so you are not surprised on arrival.
  • A road test. Federal rules require a road test before you drive for a carrier, with limited equivalents described in 49 CFR 391.31 and 391.33. Many carriers still run their own road test during or right after orientation. Ask how theirs works and what equipment you will be in.

The order of these steps varies. Some carriers finish most of them before they invite you to orientation; others do them while you are there. Ask for the sequence so you can plan.

Get These Answers in Writing Before You Go

Orientation is a bad time to learn that the pay, home time or truck is not what you were told. Before you travel, get the job terms in writing and keep a copy:

  • How you will be paid, and what is and is not paid (miles, detention, layover, breaks, or a daily or hourly rate).
  • The home-time policy for that seat, not a generic ad line.
  • The equipment you will be assigned, including whether it has an APU or inverter if that matters to you.
  • Whether orientation itself is paid, and who covers travel and lodging if you have to go out of town. That varies by carrier. Do not assume it is covered unless it is in writing.
  • What happens if you do not pass the drug test, the road test or a background check after you have already traveled.

If a recruiter dodges those questions, that is useful information. We walk through how to compare the whole offer, not just the headline number, in Florida CDL-A jobs: what pay to expect and in OTR vs. regional vs. local.

What Orientation Itself Is Usually Like

Length, location and format vary. Some carriers do it in a day at a terminal; others run several days and may include policy videos, a handbook review, a yard walk and a road test. Plan to stay for the full session they described, and ask ahead of time how long to block out and what to bring for an overnight stay if they say you will be there more than a day.

Read what you sign. If a document does not match what you were told about pay, deductions, home time, lease-purchase or the truck, stop and ask. You can decline and leave. There is no shame in walking away from a seat that is not what was described. If a lease-purchase or lease-on deal comes up, read our guide to spotting a bad lease-purchase deal before you sign.

A Note About Fees

Drivers never pay Road Ready Crew a dime — not to apply, not to get placed, and not out of your first check. If anybody claiming to be us asks you for money, it isn't us. If a carrier or a third party asks you to pay an "orientation fee" or to wire money for a hotel or a drug test, get that in writing and be cautious. Confirm it with the carrier on a number you looked up yourself, not a number a stranger texted you.
