CDL Disqualifications and MVR Basics

Your CDL is only as good as the record behind it. Carriers pull a motor vehicle record (MVR) before they hire you, and states can take your commercial privilege away for convictions listed in federal rules. Understanding those rules helps you protect your license and answer recruiter questions honestly.

This is a plain-English summary for drivers, not legal advice. The official source is 49 CFR 383.51 (Disqualification of drivers). FMCSA sets the standards; your state issues the CDL and applies the disqualification periods in the federal tables. State law can be stricter. Always check with your state licensing agency if you have a ticket or conviction.

What "Disqualified" Means

Under 383.51, a person who is required to have a commercial learner's permit (CLP) or CDL and is disqualified must not drive a commercial motor vehicle (CMV). An employer must not knowingly allow a disqualified driver to operate a CMV. The disqualification period is on top of any earlier periods. Convictions from separate incidents count separately when the rule talks about first, second or subsequent offenses, whether the conviction was in a CMV or a personal vehicle for the offenses that say so.

States Issue the CDL and Apply the Tables

FMCSA writes the minimum disqualification table. Your state driver's license agency puts the suspension or disqualification on your CDL record and decides when you can be reinstated under those federal minimums. That is why the same conviction type shows up on the MVR carriers buy from the state. If you move, the Commercial Driver's License Information System (CDLIS) is how states share that record.

Major Offenses (Table 1)

Major offenses are the ones that can take you out for a year or for life. Table 1 in 383.51 includes convictions such as:

  • Driving under the influence of alcohol as defined by state law
  • Driving under the influence of a controlled substance
  • Having an alcohol concentration of 0.04 or greater while operating a CMV
  • Refusing an alcohol test under a state's implied-consent law
  • Leaving the scene of an accident
  • Using the vehicle to commit a felony (other than the drug-trafficking and trafficking-in-persons felonies listed separately)
  • Driving a CMV when your CLP or CDL is already revoked, suspended or canceled from prior CMV violations, or you are already disqualified
  • Causing a fatality through negligent operation of a CMV

For a first conviction in a CMV, the federal table generally calls for a one-year disqualification (or three years if you were transporting hazardous materials as defined in the rule). A second major offense in a separate incident generally means a lifetime disqualification. Some lifetime cases may be eligible for state reinstatement after 10 years if you complete an approved rehabilitation program; a later major offense after that reinstatement cannot be reinstated again. Felonies involving manufacturing or distributing controlled substances, or severe forms of trafficking in persons with a CMV, are lifetime with no 10-year reinstatement path under the federal table.

Exact periods depend on whether the vehicle was a CMV or a non-CMV and whether hazmat was involved. Read the columns in Table 1 on eCFR rather than guessing from a forum post.

Serious Traffic Violations (Table 2)

Serious traffic violations do not usually end your CDL on the first ticket. They stack. Table 2 lists offenses such as:

  • Speeding 15 mph or more over the limit
  • Reckless driving as defined by state or local law
  • Improper or erratic lane changes
  • Following too closely
  • A traffic-control violation (other than parking) connected with a fatal accident
  • Driving a CMV without a CLP or CDL, without the license in your possession, or without the right class or endorsements
  • Texting while driving a CMV, or using a hand-held mobile phone while driving a CMV, when banned by state or local law

Two serious convictions in separate incidents within three years generally mean a 60-day CMV disqualification. A third or later within three years generally means 120 days. For some non-CMV convictions, the CMV disqualification only kicks in if that conviction also cost you your license or non-CMV privileges. Again, the official columns in Table 2 control.

Railroad Crossings and Out-of-Service Orders

Table 3 covers railroad-highway grade crossing violations in a CMV (failing to slow, stop or clear the crossing as required). First conviction: at least 60 days; second within three years: at least 120 days; third or later within three years: at least one year.

Table 4 covers violating a driver or vehicle out-of-service order. First conviction while hauling non-hazmat: no less than 180 days and no more than one year. Repeat convictions within ten years carry longer ranges, and hazmat or passenger-vehicle cases start with a longer first-offense range. If an officer puts you out of service, do not move the truck until you are cleared.

What Carriers See on an MVR

An MVR is your state's driving record. Carriers order it (often through a consumer-reporting process) to see license status, CDL class and endorsements, and convictions or suspensions. They also run other checks, such as the FMCSA Drug and Alcohol Clearinghouse and, for many fleets, PSP. The MVR will not invent a clean history. If you have a ticket or a past disqualification, say so up front. Recruiters would rather hear it from you than find it on the printout.

Tips that stay within the rules:

  • Know your own record. Order a personal copy from your state before you apply so there are no surprises.
  • Fight or fix errors early. Wrong identity matches and closed cases that still show open can cost you a job offer.
  • Do not drive while disqualified. That itself is a major offense under Table 1 when you are in a CMV.
  • Ask the carrier what their MVR standard is. Every company sets its own hiring bar on top of the federal minimums.
