Florida CDL-A Jobs: What Pay to Expect in 2026

If you're driving out of Florida, or thinking about it, you've probably seen job ads promising all kinds of pay. Some of those numbers are real. Some are the best week a driver ever had, dressed up as normal. The honest answer to "what does a Florida CDL-A job pay?" is that it varies by carrier, and it varies a lot.

So instead of throwing out numbers that won't match your situation, let's talk about what actually drives your pay and how to figure out what an offer is really worth.

Why Florida Is Its Own Animal

Florida freight has a reputation. The state brings in a lot of goods, from consumer products to building materials, but it doesn't ship as much back out. That imbalance can affect how freight moves, how long you wait for a load out of the state, and how carriers set up their runs. Produce seasons, port traffic, and hurricane season can also shake things up.

What that means for you: ask carriers how they handle getting you loaded out of Florida, and whether time spent waiting gets paid.

The Factors That Drive Your Pay

Freight type

What you haul matters. Dry van, reefer, flatbed, tanker, and hazmat all pay differently. Freight that takes more skill, more physical work, or more risk usually pays more. Flatbed means securement and tarping. Tanker means handling liquid surge. Reefer means watching temps and tight appointments. Each one carries its own pay expectations.

Home time

In general, the more you're out, the more chances you have to earn. OTR jobs that keep you out longer often offer more total miles. Local jobs that get you home every night can pay well too, but the structure is usually different. Decide how much home time you need, then compare jobs that actually deliver it.

Experience

Newer drivers usually start lower and move up as they build a clean record. Carriers often have pay scales that climb with experience. Ask where you'd land on that scale and what it takes to move up.

Endorsements

Tanker, hazmat, and doubles and triples endorsements can open the door to higher-paying freight. Having them doesn't guarantee more money, but it gives you more options and more room to negotiate.

Pay structure

This one trips up a lot of drivers. Here's how the common setups work:

  • Cents per mile (CPM). You're paid for miles driven. Ask whether it's practical miles or shortest-route miles, because that changes your check. Also ask what you get when you're not rolling.
  • Hourly. Common for local and some regional work. You're paid for time on the clock, which helps when you're stuck in traffic or at a dock. Ask about overtime.
  • Percentage of the load. More common with owner-operators and some flatbed or specialized carriers. Your pay rises and falls with freight rates, so ask to see what the loads actually pay.
  • Salary or day rate. Some dedicated or local jobs pay a set amount. Steady, but make sure you know what hours and duties it covers.

Accessorial pay

Detention, layovers, breakdowns, extra stops, and tarping pay can make a big difference over a year. Some carriers pay these fairly. Some make you jump through hoops. Some don't pay them at all.

Sign-On Bonuses Come With Strings

A big sign-on bonus looks great in an ad. Before it sways you, find out:

  • Is it paid all at once, or spread out over months?
  • Do you have to stay a certain amount of time to get all of it?
  • Do you have to pay any of it back if you leave early?
  • Are there safety or performance conditions attached?

A bonus that's paid out slowly and disappears if you leave isn't the same as money in your pocket. Don't let it cover for weak weekly pay.

How to Verify a Real Offer

Here's how to cut through the advertising and see what a job really pays:

  1. Get it in writing. Ask for the pay rate, structure, and any extra pay in a written offer before you go to orientation.
  2. Ask what drivers actually make. Not the top earner. The typical driver in that same seat.
  3. Ask about actual miles or hours. What do drivers on that account run in a normal week?
  4. Talk to current drivers. If you can, talk to someone doing the job right now.
  5. Read the fine print on deductions. Know what comes out of your check.
  6. Compare the whole package. Benefits, home time, equipment, and support all count.

If a recruiter dodges these questions, that's your answer.
